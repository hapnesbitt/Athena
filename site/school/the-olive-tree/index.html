Athena School EditionBook XXIII of the Odyssey

The Olive Tree

After twenty years, a stranger says he is her husband. Penelope has one test.

Time
About 8 minutes
Cast
5 speakers, one of them small
You need
A bare floor and a few chairs. No recording.

All three scripts, ready to print

What happens

Odysseus has come home and killed the men who were trying to marry his wife. The old nurse runs to tell Penelope, who does not believe it. Liars have been coming to her door for twenty years. She sits across from the man and says almost nothing, even when her son calls her heart a stone. Then she sets a trap. She tells the nurse, as if it were nothing, to move the big bed out of the bedroom. Odysseus explodes: no one can move that bed, because he built it around a living olive tree. Only he could know that. Now she runs to him.

Other names: Ithaca (ITH-uh-kuh), Athena (uh-THEE-nuh), Poseidon (puh-SY-dun). In this scene Actor is a man's name: AK-tor, the father of Penelope's maid. Any student can play any part.

Content note

  • Violence: None on stage. The killing has just happened, and the nurse describes Odysseus standing among the dead, "stained like a lion."
  • Alcohol: None.
  • Marriage: The plot turns on the couple's bed. Nothing sexual is said or shown, and the scene ends on an embrace. Homer's Book XXIII goes on past our ending: the couple go to bed together, and Penelope mentions Helen's affair. Read the passage before you hand it out.

The beat

This scene starts in silence, and the beat has to be earned. First comes one finger tapping on Penelope's chair. Later Odysseus stamps in anger as he describes the bed, and the rest of the cast joins him one at a time, like a crew at work. It stops dead when he asks whether the tree has been cut.

Count 1, 2, 3, 4. Step on 1. Clap on 2. Step on 3. Clap on 4. Start slow, with a clap about every second and a half. For more drive, add a quick second step right after 3.

Students can also drum on a desk, beatbox, or bring a beat they made themselves. Ask everyone to speak in their own voice. No one needs an accent or a “rap voice.”

No scene here needs a recording. The full Athena was built around popular songs, but this edition uses none of their titles, hooks or lyrics. Use a beat your students make.

If you want to use a real recording: staging one in a show likely needs dramatic rights from the song’s owners, and a school’s usual music license often doesn’t cover that. Check with your school first. This is general information, not legal advice.

Athena School Edition · Book XXIII of the Odyssey · about 8 minutes

The Olive Tree

Parts: Penelope, Odysseus, Eurycleia, Telemachus, The Muse, and anyone keeping the beat.

A rule down the left side means the line goes on the beat. Everything else is spoken. Line numbers are for rehearsal.

Two chairs, far apart, facing each other. Odysseus sits in one, in rags, looking at the floor. Upstage, Penelope is asleep on a third chair.

THE MUSE

Night in the palace at Ithaca. The fight is over.

The suitors lie in a heap by the gates of the court.

Up the stairs comes the old nurse, Eurycleia, laughing,

her knees moving fast for joy, her feet stumbling over each other.

EURYCLEIA

5Wake up, Penelope! Wake up, dear child!

Come see with your own eyes what you have wanted every day!

Odysseus is home! He's in this house!

And he has killed the suitors, every one!

PENELOPE

Nurse. The gods have driven you crazy. They can do that.

10Why mock me? That was the best sleep I've had

since the day he sailed for that evil city I won't name.

Any other woman in this house, I'd send her down in tears.

Your age saves you. Go.

EURYCLEIA

I'm not mocking you, child! He's the beggar!

15The stranger they all insulted in the hall!

Telemachus knew, and he kept it quiet.

Penelope jumps up and throws her arms around the old woman.

PENELOPE

Tell me the truth, then. If he's home, how?

One man, and so many of them, always in a pack?

EURYCLEIA

I didn't see. We were locked in. I only heard the groaning.

20Then your son called me, and there he stood among the dead,

stained like a lion with the blood and dirt of battle.

It would have warmed your heart.

Penelope lets go. She steps back. She goes cold.

PENELOPE

Don't gloat yet, nurse.

You know how welcome he would be. To everyone. To me.

25But this isn't true. Some god killed them, angry at their insults.

Odysseus is lost, far away.

EURYCLEIA

Your heart never believes anything!

Then here's your proof: the scar the boar gave him with its tusk.

I saw it when I washed his feet. I tried to tell you,

30and he put his hand over my mouth.

Come and see. If I'm lying, kill me.

PENELOPE

Even you can't read the gods, nurse. But let's go down.

I'll look at the dead, and at the man who killed them.

She walks the whole length of the floor and sits in the far chair, across from him. She looks. He looks at the floor. Nobody speaks. Hold it for a slow count of fifteen. It should feel too long.

THE MUSE

She sat long in silence.

35Now she looked at him, and knew him.

Now she looked at his rags, and she did not.

TELEMACHUS

Mother. Some mother you are.

Why are you sitting over there? Go to him! Ask him something!

No other woman would keep this far from a husband

40home in the twentieth year.

Your heart is harder than stone.

PENELOPE

Beat in: Just Penelope's finger, tapping the arm of her chair. One, two, three, four. No one else.

My son, I can't speak. I can't look at his face.

Twenty years of strangers have come to this place,

Every one with a story, and the story's the same:

45"I saw him. He's coming. I know him by name."

I've fed them. I've cried. I've been played for a fool.

The door stays shut, and I make the rule.

If this is Odysseus, then he and I

Have signs no one knows. So let him try.

Beat out

ODYSSEUS

50(He smiles.) Telemachus, let your mother test me. She'll know soon enough.

Right now I'm filthy and I'm in rags. That's why she won't say it's me.

THE MUSE

They took him to the bath. They put a clean cloak on him,

and Athena poured grace over his head and shoulders,

as a master lays gold over silver.

55He came back looking like a god, and sat down again across from his wife.

She did not move.

ODYSSEUS

(The smile is gone.) Strange woman.

The gods gave you a heart nobody can soften.

No other wife alive would keep away from a husband,

60after all that, home in the twentieth year.

Nurse. Make me up a bed. I'll sleep alone.

Her heart is iron.

Penelope stands. This is the test. She says it lightly, and she does not look at him.

PENELOPE

Strange man.

I'm not proud. I'm not scornful. I'm not amazed.

65I remember very well what you looked like, sailing out of Ithaca.

Eurycleia, make up his bed.

Take the big bed out of our room, the one he built himself.

Move it out here. Put fleeces on it, and blankets.

THE MUSE

So she spoke, and tested her husband.

70The test is hers. She decides when this reunion happens, not him.

ODYSSEUS

(He is on his feet.) WHAT did you say?

Who moved my bed?

That bed can't be moved. Not by any man alive, however strong.

Only if a god came down.

75Because I made that bed. I made it, and no one else.

Beat in: He stamps it himself. Then the others take it up, one by one. Hammer and adze.

That bed's my own design,

An olive tree its spine.

An olive grew inside our wall,

Long in the leaf, like a pillar, tall.

80I didn't cut it. I laid the stone,

I built our room around it, alone.

Then I cut the branches, trimmed the crown,

Left the trunk with its root still driven down.

Smoothed it, trued it, drilled it, and there:

85That's our bedpost. It goes nowhere.

Gold and silver and ivory laid,

Purple oxhide, tight. I made

That bed with these hands. That was my sign.

So tell me, woman: is that bed still mine?

90Or did some man come with a saw,

Cut through that trunk, and move it all?

Beat out: Dead stop.

Silence. Penelope's knees give way. Then she runs, the whole length of the floor, and puts her arms around his neck.

PENELOPE

Don't be angry with me, Odysseus.

You were always the wisest of men.

It was the gods who gave us sorrow.

95They could not bear to let us stay together and be young,

and come to the door of old age side by side.

Don't be angry that I didn't run to you when I first saw you.

All this time my heart has been shaking,

afraid some man would come and fool me with his words.

100There are so many.

But you have told me the sign of our bed.

No one has ever seen it but you, and me, and one maid,

Actor's daughter, who kept our door.

There. You have won it, my stubborn heart.

Odysseus weeps. They hold on.

THE MUSE

105As when the sight of land is welcome to swimmers,

whose ship Poseidon has smashed on the open sea,

and only a few escape the grey water and swim for shore,

their bodies crusted with salt, glad to set foot on land:

so welcome to her was the sight of her husband,

110and her white arms she would not let go from his neck.

And rosy-fingered Dawn would have found them weeping still,

but grey-eyed Athena held the night back at the edge of the west,

and would not let the morning harness her horses.

Three prompts

  1. Rehearse. Hold the first silence for a full count of fifteen, then twenty-five. What do the two actors do with it? Try it with Penelope looking at him and with Penelope looking away. Which makes the audience lean in?
  2. Discuss. Is Penelope's test fair, or is it a trick? Why does his anger prove who he is better than the scar or his story could? What makes a secret work like a password?
  3. Discuss. The last speech compares someone to a shipwrecked swimmer reaching land. Odysseus is the one who was shipwrecked, but the comparison is about Penelope. Why would Homer give that image to her? What does it say about her twenty years?

The matching passage of Homer

Read Book XXIII, from the nurse on the stairs to the night Athena holds back (https://athena.arc-codex.com/school/homer/book-23.html), in Butcher and Lang’s public-domain translation.

Where this scene differs from Homer

  • We cut Odysseus's plan to stage a fake wedding dance so the town won't hear about the killings yet, and his talk with Telemachus about what to do next.
  • We end before the prophecy of Teiresias, the couple's night and the stories they tell each other.
  • Penelope's verse about lying strangers is ours, built from one line in this book, where she says she feared being fooled by men's words, and from Book XIV, where the swineherd says wanderers keep bringing her false news.
  • The beat stopping and starting is our staging. The test, the bed, and the swimmer comparison are all Homer's.
