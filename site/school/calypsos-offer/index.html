Calypso's Offer

A goddess offers a man eternal life. He would rather go home and grow old.

Time
About 8 minutes
Cast
4 speakers
You need
A bare floor and a few chairs. No recording.

All three scripts, ready to print

What happens

For seven years the goddess Calypso has kept Odysseus on her island. Zeus sends Hermes to order her to let him go. She is furious, and she has a real complaint: gods take mortal lovers whenever they like, but a goddess who does the same gets her man killed. She obeys anyway. She offers Odysseus one last chance to stay and live forever. He says he knows she is more beautiful than his wife, and he wants to go home all the same. She helps him build a raft and sends him off, and Poseidon sees him leave.

Orion (oh-RY-un), Artemis (AR-tuh-mis), Demeter (dih-MEE-ter), Iasion (eye-AY-zee-on), Laertes (lay-UR-teez), Styx (STICKS), Phaeacians (fee-AY-shunz), Penelope (puh-NEL-uh-pee). Any student can play any part.

Content note

  • Violence: None on stage. Calypso's speech names two men killed by the gods, and the scene ends on the threat of Poseidon.
  • Alcohol: None in the scene. Hermes is served nectar, the drink of the gods. Homer's Calypso also packs wine for the raft, and we left that out.
  • Being held against your will: Calypso keeps Odysseus on the island and says she loves him. The script goes no further than "I loved him and kept him." Homer goes further: he says Calypso holds Odysseus by force and that he slept beside her at night as an "unwilling lover." The matching passage includes that, so read it before you hand it out.

The beat

Each character makes a different beat. Hermes snaps his fingers, quick and light. Calypso stamps her own, slow and angry, and later taps one finger on her chair. Odysseus only walks. When the raft gets built, everyone chops on counts one and three.

Count 1, 2, 3, 4. Step on 1. Clap on 2. Step on 3. Clap on 4. Start slow, with a clap about every second and a half. For more drive, add a quick second step right after 3.

Students can also drum on a desk, beatbox, or bring a beat they made themselves. Ask everyone to speak in their own voice. No one needs an accent or a “rap voice.”

No scene here needs a recording. The full Athena was built around popular songs, but this edition uses none of their titles, hooks or lyrics. Use a beat your students make.

If you want to use a real recording: staging one in a show likely needs dramatic rights from the song’s owners, and a school’s usual music license often doesn’t cover that. Check with your school first. This is general information, not legal advice.

Calypso's Offer

Parts: Calypso, Odysseus, Hermes, The Muse, and anyone keeping the beat.

A rule down the left side means the line goes on the beat. Everything else is spoken. Line numbers are for rehearsal.

Upstage, Calypso walks to and fro before a loom made of air, humming. Far downstage, Odysseus sits with his back to all of it, looking out over the audience. The audience is the sea. He hears nothing until she comes to him.

THE MUSE

Seven years on one island, in the navel of the sea.

All the others who came through Troy are home by now, or dead.

This one sits on the rocks and weeps, and the nymph Calypso, that fair goddess, holds him there.

But grey-eyed Athena has spoken for him on Olympus.

5So Zeus, gatherer of the clouds, has sent his messenger.

HERMES

Beat inQuick and light. Finger snaps. Hermes sets the speed.

Gold on my feet and the wind at my back,

Skim the wave like a cormorant, stay on track.

Long way. Salt. More salt. Still salt.

No town. No temple. That's Zeus's fault.

10Not one burnt offering for miles and miles.

Zeus said go, so I go, with style.

I'm Hermes, the messenger. That's no boast,

Just stating the news on a lonely coast.

Beat out

He sees the cave. He stops.

Okay.

15Cedar on the fire. I could smell it from the beach.

Four springs running. Violets. A vine around the door.

Even a god could stand here and be glad.

He stands there and is glad.

Okay. Work.

CALYPSO

She does not stop weaving.

Hermes of the golden wand.

20Welcome. And what an honor. You never come here.

Gods are no strangers to each other, so say it.

If I can do it, I will. But sit. Eat first.

She sets out ambrosia and nectar. He eats fast.

HERMES

You asked, so here it is, god to goddess.

Zeus sent me. I did not volunteer.

25Who would fly across all that on purpose?

You have a man here, the unluckiest of all who fought at Troy.

His crew is lost. The wind and the wave brought him to you.

Zeus says: send him home, and quickly.

It is not his fate to die far from his people.

30And no god gets around Zeus. That's all there is to say.

The loom stops. Calypso shudders. Then she stamps, once, twice. It becomes her beat.

CALYPSO

Beat inHer own stamp, slow and hard. Others join in with claps.

Hard. You gods are hard. And jealous, too.

A god takes a lover? Not a word from you.

But a goddess loves a man in the light of day?

You can't stand it. You take him away.

 

35Dawn chose Orion. Artemis shot him.

Demeter chose Iasion. Zeus's bolt got him.

Now it's my turn, and he sends his boy,

Like I stole this man from the walls of Troy.

 

Who saved him? Me. Zeus threw the bolt

40That split his ship. So whose is the fault?

Alone on a keel, half drowned, half dead:

I pulled him in and I gave him bread.

You call this a prison. I call it a shore

Where a drowned man's breathing. Who did more?

45I loved him and kept him. I gave him my word:

No death, no old age. Best offer he's heard.

Beat out

She picks up the shuttle.

But no god gets around Zeus. Fine.

Let him go, over the sea that gives no harvest.

I have no ships. I have no crew. I won't send him.

50But I'll tell him how to get home safe, and I will hide nothing.

HERMES

Good. Do it. And mind Zeus's temper. He remembers.

He is gone.

THE MUSE

She went down to the shore and found him where he always was,

his eyes never dry, his sweet life running out,

looking across the sea that gives no harvest.

55She had stopped pleasing him long ago. He had no ship, no crew, no way to leave.

CALYPSO

Poor man. Stop crying. Don't let your life drain away on this beach.

I'm sending you home, with all my heart.

Get up. Cut long beams. Build a wide raft and deck it high.

I'll load it with bread and water. I'll put clothes on your back

60and send a good wind behind you.

ODYSSEUS

He gets up slowly. He doesn't trust it.

No. What's the plan here, goddess?

A raft? On that? Good ships with a god's own wind don't like that crossing.

I'm not setting foot on any raft unless you swear to me,

a great oath, that you're planning nothing against me.

Calypso smiles.

CALYPSO

65You rogue. Only you would think of that.

All right. Let Earth be my witness, and wide Heaven above,

and the falling water of the Styx, the greatest oath the gods can swear:

I plan no harm to you.

I'm telling you what I would tell myself, if I were where you are.

70I know what's right, too. My heart is not iron.

Two chairs, facing. He sits in the one Hermes left. They eat. Nobody speaks.

CALYPSO

Beat inJust her fingers on the arm of the chair. Slow.

Son of Laertes, you really want home?

Today? Right now? Then go. Go alone.

But if you knew what the sea has in store,

You'd stay in this house and you'd ask for no more.

75No death. No old age. Think it through.

I know you miss her. I've watched you, too,

Every day at the edge of the sea.

Is she taller? Is she finer than me?

Look at me, man, and answer me. Say.

80A mortal, next to a goddess? No way.

Beat out

He takes his time.

ODYSSEUS

Goddess. Queen. Don't be angry with me.

I know all that. I know it well.

Penelope is wise, but next to you she is nothing to look at.

She will grow old. She will die. You never will.

A pause.

85And still. Every day, I want to go home.

Every day.

Beat inHis own feet. One step at a time.

If some god wrecks me on the wine-dark sea,

I'll take it. I've got that heart in me.

Waves and war, I've had my share.

90Add this to the pile. Put it there.

Beat out

THE MUSE

So soon as early Dawn shone forth, the rosy-fingered,

she gave him an axe of bronze, two-edged, with a handle of olive wood,

and she led him to the edge of the island, where the tall trees grew.

ODYSSEUS

Beat inThe work. Everyone who isn't speaking chops, on counts one and three.

Twenty trees: alder, poplar, pine.

95Cut them, trim them, make straight the line.

Calypso comes with augers. She sets them down where he can reach them. She goes. She says nothing.

Bore every beam and peg it tight.

Deck it and rail it, stem to stern, right.

Mast. Yard. A rudder to steer.

Calypso comes with cloth for the sails. She sets it down. She goes.

Sails I can sew. I'm nearly clear.

100Four days' work, and she's built to float.

Lever her down to the sea. That's my boat.

Beat out

CALYPSO

On the fifth day. She puts a cloak around him. She hands him a bag and a skin.

Water. Grain. The food you like.

She lifts her hand. A warm wind.

Steer by the Bear. Keep it on your left.

She has more to say. She doesn't say it.

Go.

He raises the sail. He is glad. It's the first time we have seen him glad. He doesn't look back. Calypso watches him a while, then walks to the loom, picks up the gold shuttle and starts to hum.

THE MUSE

105Ten days and seven he sailed, and sleep never fell on his eyes.

On the eighteenth day the shadowy hills of the Phaeacians rose up,

like a shield on the misty sea.

And far off, on his way home from the Ethiopians,

the lord who shakes the earth looked up and saw him.

One stomp, everyone. Stop.

Three prompts

  1. Rehearse. Stage the supper with the two chairs close together, then far apart, then side by side and facing the sea. What does each choice say about these two? Keep the one that makes his answer hurt the most.
  2. Discuss. Calypso calls the island a shelter for a drowned man. Odysseus weeps on its shore every day, and Homer says she holds him by force. Whose version do you believe? Can both be true? Use the lines.
  3. Discuss. He turns down eternal life for a wife who will age and a rough little island. What does he want that a goddess cannot give him? Would you make the same choice?

The matching passage of Homer

Read Book V, from the council of the gods to the moment Poseidon sees the raft (https://athena.arc-codex.com/school/homer/book-05.html), in Butcher and Lang’s public-domain translation.

Where this scene differs from Homer

  • The council of the gods is told in one line. As in Homer, Hermes speaks only to Calypso and never meets Odysseus.
  • Calypso's idea of the island as a shelter, and the direct charge that male gods get away with it, are ours. Homer's Calypso says the gods are jealous of goddesses who take mortal lovers, and she leaves the rest unsaid.
  • Homer says the two spend the last night together. We leave that out and go straight from his answer to the morning.
  • Her silent trips with the tools are our staging, but the tools are Homer's: she brings the augers and the sailcloth herself.
